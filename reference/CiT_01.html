01

Data Movement Is the Bottleneck

LLM work moves large, regular blocks of data between devices. Two of them set the scale for this series: the gradient or pseudo-gradient sync in training, and the KV-cache hand-off in disaggregated inference.

Training: one model-sized update

  • Llama-3-70B, BF16: 141.10 GB per update.
  • A ring all-reduce sends 246.93 GB from each of 8 workers, 277.80 GB from each of 64.
  • Moving it once takes 313.6 ms over NVLink 4, 2,822.1 ms over InfiniBand NDR and 11,288.4 ms over 100 GbE.

Inference: one prompt's KV hand-off

  • Llama-3-8B, 2,048 tokens: 268.4 MB (131,072 bytes per token).
  • 0.60 ms over NVLink 4, 5.38 ms over IB NDR, 21.49 ms over 100 GbE, 85.92 ms over 25 GbE.
  • A 32,768-token Llama-3-70B prompt hands off 10,737.4 MB: 859.01 ms over 100 GbE.

  • The training sync is more than 500 times the size of a short-prompt KV hand-off. Over a cross-data-centre 100 GbE link, a Llama-3-70B update takes 11.3 s uncompressed. That is the traffic DiLoCo-family methods shrink, and it is where compression in transit would act.
  • Weight streaming is the third movement: Llama-3-70B's 141.10 GB of BF16 weights take 2.20 s over PCIe Gen5 x16.
  • Size alone does not make a bottleneck. Whether the link bounds the system is the first of the five tests (slide 10).
02

What Compute in Transit Means

Compute in transit is computation applied in the data path, on data that must move anyway. The data is not fetched to a processor and sent back; it is changed on the way past. Where the computation sits decides what it can see.

PlacementSeesCan doCannot do
Endpoint (GPU, NIC)Its own data, before it leaves or after it landsAnything the device's instruction set allows, at the cost of its own time and memory trafficCombine with data it has not received; it is not “free”, since it competes with the device's main work
Link (pluggable transceiver module)One flow, at line ratePer-stream transforms: compress, re-encode, transform, score against a held vectorCombine two streams: a sum of gradients from two workers needs both at one point
SwitchMany flowsCombine flows: in-network aggregation (the sum in an all-reduce)Much per-byte work: the same compute budget is shared by every port

A photonic interconnect that only moves bytes is not compute in transit. The distinction in this series is whether the device changes the data (glossary).

03

The Photonic Link Module

The series starts from one published device. Kundu, Hammond, Todd, Goel, Simpson, Rajendra, Michel, Crawford, Bergamaschi, R. Todd and New describe it in “Programmable Compute-in-Transit using Integrated Photonics” (arXiv:2608.21536, submitted 21 August 2026).

What the paper reports

  • A QSFP56 pluggable module with an integrated optical Fourier transform device.
  • It lists radix-4 FFT and NTT, addition, subtraction, multiplication, MAC, floating-point MAC and modular arithmetic. The modular logic runs in the driver IP on the host FPGA, an AMD Alveo V80 attached through the QSFP56 port.
  • “Tens of GFLOPs of operations on data links”: the authors' claim.
  • Evaluated inside a CKKS bootstrapping flow: ring dimension 65,536, 32-bit words, about 420 GB streamed through the photonics; functional correctness checked against a CPU implementation. The 65,536-point NTTs were split into 256-point transforms, then spread over four optical channels by radix-4 decomposition.
  • The photonic devices run at 14 GBaud, with NRZ data into the module; module power is 9.7 W. No throughput, latency or energy per operation is measured.

What the developer says

  • Compute-in-Transit is “operations performed with light directly in the data path”, and “AI infrastructure has hit the data movement wall” (the developer’s website).
  • Estimated 2.76 pJ/bit link energy on 45 nm silicon photonics, excluding the laser; “Native 384 GOPS / lambda @ 32GSps (GF45nm)” (the developer’s website).
  • Evaluation kit: photonic compute module, AMD Alveo V80 accelerator, QSFP data path and control software.
  • Stated targets: distributed and hyperscale AI, optical interconnect, secure and confidential AI; AI, security, HPC and telecoms (the developer’s website). The paper adds post-quantum cryptography and FHE.
How much weight these figures carry

A third-party review notes that the GFLOPs figure is asserted rather than measured (pith.science). The GOPS and pJ/bit figures are company estimates. This deck treats all three as claims and brackets “tens of GFLOPs” with 10 and 99 GFLOP/s, which is our reading, not a measurement.

04

The Published Operation Set, and What Is Missing

The paper lists radix-4 FFT and NTT, addition and subtraction, multiplication, MAC, floating-point MAC and modular arithmetic (add, subtract, multiply, MAC, MSUB). Everything a candidate kernel does must be built from them, or it cannot run in the module.

OperationListed?What it gives an LLM kernel
FFT (1×N, radix-4)yesFrequency-domain compression along a sequence or within a chunk
NTT (1×N, radix-4)yesExact transforms over integers mod q: FHE, not float traffic
Modular arithmeticyesInteger rescaling and encoding (runs in the host FPGA’s driver IP)
Addition, subtraction, multiplicationyesSumming streams, scaling, element-wise products
MAC, floating-point MACyesDot products against a held vector, weighted sums
Comparisonnot listedNeeded for absmax scaling, thresholds, max
Rounding to a narrower formatnot listedNeeded to requantise BF16 to FP8
Top-k selectionnot listedNeeded by DeMo-style sparsification and KV block selection
Data-dependent controlnot listedNeeded for any “if this value, then” logic

“Not listed” means only that the public sources do not mention it; the module or its host FPGA may do more.

Consequence

Kernels must be data-independent: the same arithmetic on every value, whatever its size. Keeping a fixed frequency band replaces top-k. On synthetic 64×64 chunks, keeping 1/16 of the DCT coefficients, the fixed band keeps 0.957 of a smooth signal's energy against top-k's 0.995, but only 0.062 of white noise against top-k's 0.325 (results.md, section 5, illustrative).

05

How the Optics Computes

A lens performs a Fourier transform on a coherent light field as it propagates, and two lenses with a mask between them compute a convolution (the 4f system). The transform has no operation count, only the cost of getting data into and out of the light. The background is in Fourier Optics for Inference 01: the lens as a Fourier transformer and the 4f system.

Electrical bits in → Modulators → Passive diffractive OFT device
4 optical channels → Detectors → Digital recombination
larger, higher precision
  • A passive device on the chip. The photonic chip carries Mach-Zehnder modulators, “a proprietary passive diffractive device that computes Optical Fourier Transform (OFT)”, and balanced photodiodes for coherent detection (Kundu et al., section II).
  • Small transforms, recombined. The prototype has four optical channels and builds larger transforms by radix-4 decomposition: in the FHE run, 65,536-point NTTs were split into 256-point transforms and spread over the four channels (Kundu et al., section IV).
  • Pending patents (the module’s developer, priority 10 September 2024, checked on Google Patents): “Arbitrary size and precision Fourier transforms via optical Fourier transforms” (GB-2643957-A, WO-2026057669-A1) and the same for number-theoretic transforms (GB-2643956-A, WO-2026057670-A1).

So the native optical transform is small, and long transforms are built by recombination. How much of that recombination is digital, and whether it counts against the module's “tens of GFLOPs”, is not public. That question decides the budget (slide 07).

Port rate is not the optics' rate

25 GB/s is the QSFP56 port's rate. In the prototype the port connects the module to its host FPGA, and the photonic devices run at 14 GBaud, with NRZ data into four modulators (Kundu et al., section II): about 7 GB/s, 28% of the port, by our arithmetic. At 7 GB/s the same claim gives 1.43 to 14.14 operations per byte, enough for a charged BF16 FFT of length 2,048 at the high end. That is more work per byte, but on a link the prototype could not fill: a module limited to its optics' rate would itself throttle a 200 Gb/s link. 25 GB/s is the rate a product would have to match.

07

Is the Transform Free?

There are two ways to account for the optical transform, and the answer to the whole question changes with the choice.

Charged

The “tens of GFLOPs” include the transform's FLOPs (or the digital recombination that builds long transforms from short ones). Every FFT costs 2.5 log2 n per value. At the low end no transform fits: even length 2 costs 1.25 per BF16 byte against a budget of 0.40. At the high end, 3.96 per byte, the longest BF16 FFT that fits is length 8.

Free

The optics performs the transform by propagation, and the GFLOPs figure counts only the other arithmetic. Then every transform-based kernel fits, and the remaining arithmetic decides. A fixed-band selection is a gather with no arithmetic, so it fits even at the low end.

  • The verdict turns on one unknown. Charged, only short transforms and light arithmetic fit. Free, every transform-based kernel fits.
  • Requantising to FP8 fits the high budget arithmetically, but it needs rounding and comparison, which are not in the published set.
  • This is the first open question for the module’s developer (slide 12).

Precision: Noise or Exactness

An optical transform is analogue. Its result carries noise from the light, the detectors and the converters. Whether that matters depends on the data.

Float traffic: a noise level

  • Gradients, activations and KV are floating-point. They already carry their format's rounding error, so the analogue chain need only match it, not be exact.
  • The rule from FOptInf 01, slide 17: relative RMS error = crest × 2−ENOB × 2/√12. Matching BF16 needs about 10.3 ENOB, INT8 8.0, FP8 E4M3 6.3 (FOptInf results.md, section 6).
  • Compressed traffic is lossy anyway. Analogue noise adds to the compression error; it does not need to vanish.

Cryptography: exact results

  • FHE and post-quantum schemes work in integers mod q. A single wrong digit corrupts the ciphertext.
  • The module reaches exactness by decomposition: small, low-precision optical transforms recombined digitally into larger, higher-precision ones (the patents on slide 05).
  • The CKKS bootstrapping evaluation checked functional correctness against a CPU implementation (slide 03).
Why this matters for LLM traffic

Decomposition buys exactness with extra passes and digital work. Float traffic does not need it, so an LLM kernel could, in principle, run the optics at its native precision. The developer claims “Scalable accuracy e.g. FP4->FP64” and the paper projects FP4 MAC on a future link, but no ENOB or error figure is published; the driver IP includes a proprietary error-correction block. The FHE accelerator series works through the exact case (FHESim 04).

Simulator presets: disagg_sim/hardware.py, LINKS. They are first-order coefficients for the simulator's energy model, not measurements.

  • The scopes differ. The simulator's figures mean end to end (SerDes, NIC, switch); the module’s figure is a link estimate without the laser. The comparison says the module's estimate is in the range of an optical link, not that it saves energy over one.
  • What would save energy is moving fewer bits. A kernel that compresses a stream by a factor k cuts the downstream bits by k, and that saving dwarfs any difference between these coefficients, provided the compression is worth having.
  • The laser, the host FPGA and any digital recombination add to the module's bill. None is in the 2.76 pJ/bit.

5PlacementDoes it need one flow (a pluggable) or many (a switch)?Slide 02
Test 1 in Brendan's simulator

Disaggregated_Inference_Sim compresses the KV hand-off either in transit or on the prefill GPU (FOptInf 03, slide 13). Compression helped only where the link bound the system: GQA KV over 25 GbE at 14 req/s, FP8 cut the hand-off p99 from 9,657.8 ms to 166.6 ms and lifted SLO attainment from 41.0% to 100.0%. The GPU did the same compression for the same 166.6 ms (simulator results.md, section 15; transit stage parameters illustrative).

  • Test 1 comes first. If the link is idle half the time, compressing its traffic changes nothing a user sees.
  • Doing it in transit must also beat doing it at the endpoint. A GPU can often do the same elementwise work nearly free; the module wins only where the endpoint is busy, cannot see the data, or the data is encrypted.
11

Candidates at a Glance

Each data movement placed on two of the tests. The placement is a judgement from this deck's arithmetic, not a measurement; decks 02 and 03 test it.

  • Strong: large, slow transfers whose compression can be data-independent. Training syncs over cross-data-centre links are the clearest case (11.3 s per Llama-3-70B update over 100 GbE). Deck 02 covers training; deck 03 covers inference (KV tiering, query-aware selection, hand-off).
  • Needs a switch: the in-cluster all-reduce is bound by traffic, but summing two workers' streams needs both at one point.
  • Fits, little to gain: the operations suit encrypted traffic and ring-attention KV blocks, but those transfers rarely bound the system: FHE is compute-bound, and ring attention already hides its transfers behind compute (deck 03).
  • Weak: pipeline- and tensor-parallel activations are latency-critical, poorly tolerant of noise (forward-pass error compounds), and already on the fastest links; weight streaming has no useful lossless kernel and matters only at cold start.

What to Take Away

  • Compute in transit changes data that must move anyway. A pluggable sees one flow, a switch sees many; only a switch can sum two streams.
  • The budget is compute rate over line rate. On a QSFP56 link the module's claim gives 0.40 to 3.96 operations per byte; faster links shrink it. At the prototype optics' own rate, about 7 GB/s, it gives 1.43 to 14.14, but that rate cannot fill a 200 Gb/s link.
  • Whether the transform is free decides everything. Charged, nothing longer than an 8-point BF16 FFT fits; free, every fixed-band kernel fits.
  • The published operations are FFT, NTT, MAC, and modular and floating-point arithmetic. Without comparison or rounding, kernels must be data-independent: a fixed band instead of top-k.
  • The largest movements are training syncs: 141.10 GB per Llama-3-70B BF16 update, against 268.4 MB for a 2,048-token Llama-3-8B KV hand-off.
  • Test 1 comes first. Compressing traffic helps only where the link bounds the system, and then the endpoint can often do it too.
