Why Fourier Optics, and Why Now

A lens performs a two-dimensional Fourier transform on a light field as the light propagates through it. Every pixel of the transform is produced at once, by physics rather than by arithmetic, so the transform has no O(n log n) operation count, only the cost of getting data into and out of the light. On a chip the same physics works in one dimension: light spreading through a slab, or a fixed mesh of couplers, transforms the values on its input waveguides (slide 13). That asymmetry is the whole story of this series.

Where the cost sits. Every value pays a conversion on the way in and out; the transform in the middle is done by propagation. Slides 17–20 put numbers on the edges.

What it offers

  • Parallelism or speed: in free space a megapixel field is transformed in one pass; on a chip a new vector enters every modulator symbol, at GHz rates (slide 13).
  • A free convolution: two lenses and a mask (the 4f system, slide 05) multiply in the frequency domain; on a chip the mask becomes a row of attenuators or modulators between two transforms (slide 14).
  • Low energy per operation inside the optics; the bill is paid at the edges (slide 20).

What it demands

  • A workload whose expensive part is a Fourier transform or a convolution. Deck 02 checks whether LLM inference has one.
  • Tolerance of analogue precision (slide 17).
  • Enough reuse per conversion to amortise the DACs, ADCs, lasers and modulators.

The Words Optics Uses

The rest of the deck leans on a dozen terms from optics. For an engineer, most have a signal-processing twin: light is a carrier, the field at a point is a complex number (a phasor), and a spatial frequency is a frequency across space instead of time.

Four pictures behind every later slide. (a) A wave and its wavelength, amplitude and phase. (b) A plane wave: flat wavefronts, parallel rays. (c) A lens turns a plane wave into one converging on its focal point, one focal length f behind it. (d) Waves add as fields: in phase they reinforce, half a cycle apart they cancel.

Where spatial frequency comes from (tiltRipple). A plane wave meets a plane (right) at angle θ. Its wavefronts cross the plane one wavelength apart along the ray, so along the plane the field ripples with period λ/sinθ: a spatial frequency u = sinθ/λ cycles per metre. Head-on (θ = 0) the field is the same everywhere: u = 0, the DC term. Turned round, this says any pattern on a plane is a sum of plane waves leaving at the angles its frequencies dictate: what a lens sorts in 04. Angles exaggerated.

The Lens as a Fourier Transformer

Place a transparency with field U0(x, y) in the front focal plane of a thin lens of focal length f, and illuminate it with a coherent plane wave of wavelength λ. In the back focal plane the field is, in the paraxial (Fresnel) approximation:

Uf(u, v) = 1⁄iλf ∬ U0(x, y) · exp[−i 2π (xu + yv) / (λf)] dx dy

  • A Fourier transform, evaluated at spatial frequency (fx, fy) = (u, v) / (λf). A detector pixel at distance u from the axis reads the input's component at frequency u/(λf): longer focal lengths spread the spectrum out.
  • Front focal plane matters. With the input elsewhere, the transform picks up a quadratic phase factor; intensity measurements do not see it, but a following stage that uses the phase does.
  • Resolution is finite. The lens aperture and the pixel pitch of the devices at each plane bound the number of resolvable points (the space–bandwidth product), which plays the role of the FFT length.
  • It is 2-D by nature. A cylindrical lens transforms along one axis only, so one device can compute many independent 1-D transforms, one per row, in parallel. That is the form a sequence model's per-channel convolutions would take (deck 02).

Left: the input field U0 (128 × 128 samples). Right: what a camera in the back focal plane sees, |Uf|² on a log scale, computed by a 2-D FFT of the left panel (centred: zero frequency on the axis). A square gives a sinc² cross, a circle the Airy rings, two slits fringes, a grating a row of spots. Small features spread far; large ones stay near the axis.

The relation is the standard result of scalar diffraction theory, derived in any Fourier-optics text; the review by Wetzstein et al. puts it in the context of optical inference (Nature 588, 2020).

04

Where the Fourier Plane Is: 2f and 4f

Any input field is a sum of plane waves, one per spatial frequency u, each leaving at its own angle (sinθ = λu). A lens maps every direction to one point of its back focal plane, so each component lands on its own spot:

x = f tanθ ≈ λ f u

Paraxial ray transfer, traced by the model (trace4f): every ray of slope s reaches height f·s in the Fourier plane, whatever its starting height, and leaves the output at height −y0. Five components (u = −2u0 … 2u0); angles exaggerated for visibility. Each colour is one plane-wave component, a bundle of parallel rays from the whole input, not a single input point.

Why that point holds the transform: path lengths

Light from every input point reaches every point of the Fourier plane. The paths are millions of wavelengths long; only their differences, as fractions of a wavelength, matter, because they set the phase each contribution arrives with.

  • On the axis (DC) the lens makes every path the same length, so every contribution arrives in phase and the field is the plain sum of the input.
  • Off the axis, at height x, the path from input height y is shorter by x·y/f. That difference grows steadily across the input and wraps through a whole wavelength every λf/x of input height.
  • The contributions add up (interfere constructively) only if the input ripples with that same period, spatial frequency u = x/(λf); for any other frequency they cancel. The field at x therefore measures how much of frequency u the input holds: the Fourier transform. With pixels at the input and the detector, each such point becomes a DFT bin (slide 11).

Twelve input points with amplitude 1 + cos(2π·2y/D), a ripple of two cycles across the input (bars), and one Fourier-plane point stepped from the axis outwards. Each dial is that point's extra path from the input point, one turn per wavelength; each arrow is that point's contribution, its length the amplitude and its angle the phase. The sum (dashed) is DFT bin k of the input, checked in the tests (pathPhasors): 12 at DC, 6 at 2u0, 0 elsewhere.

2f: one lens, the spectrum

Input one focal length in front of the lens, read one focal length behind it. That back focal plane holds the far-field (Fraunhofer) pattern: the Fourier transform of the input. Put a camera there to read the spectrum, or a mask to filter it.

4f: two lenses, back to an image

A second lens transforms again. Two forward transforms return the input mirrored (y → −y), multiplied by whatever the mask did in between: a filtered image. That is the convolver of slide 05.

05

The 4f System and the Convolution Theorem

The four planes, each computed: lens 1 = a forward 2-D FFT; the mask multiplies; lens 2 = a second forward FFT, so the output comes back mirrored (look at the shapes' positions). With the mask open the output equals the mirrored input to rounding, a check browser_check runs.

  • The convolution theorem: F{h ∗ u} = F{h} · F{u}. Lens 1 transforms the input, the mask multiplies it by the filter's spectrum H, and lens 2 transforms again. A second forward transform returns the result with coordinates mirrored, which is a relabelling, not a cost.
  • Correlation is the same machine with the conjugate spectrum on the mask: the classical optical correlator, used for pattern matching.
  • The mask holds the weights. Every different filter needs a different mask, so how fast and how large the mask can be rewritten matters as much as the transform (slide 09; deck 02 counts the rewrites for a sequence model).
  • Digital equivalent: FFT, pointwise multiply, inverse FFT, in O(n log n). An FFT computes a circular convolution; a causal, linear one needs zero-padding to at least twice the length, as the next slide shows.

Learned 4f layers have been demonstrated for image classification, with an optimised diffractive element as the mask (Chang et al., Sci. Rep. 8, 2018), and an amplitude-only Fourier-plane processor runs convolutions on about 1,000×1,000 matrices (Miscuglio et al., arXiv:2008.05853; Optica 2020).

Scale and Coherence

Because spatial frequency u lands at x = λ f u, the same input gives a pattern whose size scales with wavelength. One wavelength gives one sharp pattern; several give differently scaled copies that smear into each other, worst at high frequencies.

1,550 nm

An amplitude grating (period p = 20 µm, 64 periods) behind a lens of f = 100 mm: intensity across the back focal plane, log scale, from the grating's FFT read at u = x/(λf) (intensityAt). The first order sits at x1 = λf/p. Three wavelengths (450, 532 and 633 nm) make three patterns whose orders separate further at each order. Grating and lens are illustrative.

Temporal coherence

One wavelength (a narrow-linewidth laser), so every frequency lands in one place. Broadband light blurs the spectrum radially.

Spatial coherence

A collimated beam (a single plane wave in) so the input's own structure, not the source's, sets the pattern. An extended source convolves the spectrum with its own angular spread.

Incoherent light

Still convolves, but intensities, with the intensity point-spread function: a real, non-negative kernel and no field Fourier transform (slide 08).

08

Coherent and Incoherent Light

Two equal beams, relative phase φ stepped round a full cycle (twoBeam). Coherent: the fields add as arrows, and the intensity |1 + eiφ|² swings from 4 to 0: signed values can cancel. Incoherent: intensities add, always 2.

Coherent (a laser)

  • Fields add as complex amplitudes: two beams can cancel. Values can be signed or complex, encoded in amplitude and phase.
  • The lens, or an on-chip transform, acts on the field, so the 4f system computes a true complex convolution.
  • Costs: a stable laser, phase-accurate modulators, alignment, and speckle and coherent noise from every stray reflection.

Incoherent (LEDs, broadband light)

  • Intensities add, and intensities are never negative.
  • An incoherent system convolves intensity with the intensity point-spread function: a real, non-negative kernel only.
  • Signed values need a bias or a split into positive and negative parts, each costing dynamic range or a second pass.
  • Amplitude-only processing is a middle way: Miscuglio et al. modulate only amplitude in both planes and report robustness to coherence noise, with orders-of-magnitude lower delay than liquid-crystal phase devices (arXiv:2008.05853).
  • For inference, activations and filters are signed, so either the system is coherent and the detector recovers sign (slide 10), or every signed product is paid for twice. The simulator design for deck 03 makes this a parameter.

Spatial Light Modulators

Data enters a free-space system through a spatial light modulator (SLM): a pixel array that writes amplitude or phase into the beam. One sits at the input plane; another can hold the Fourier-plane mask.

Pixels × rate is the throughput either way. Free space: millions of slow pixels. On chip: a few fast channels. Rates as in the table below (Miscuglio et al.; Feldmann et al.).

DeviceWritesUpdate rateNotes
Liquid-crystal SLM (e.g. LCoS)Phase (or amplitude), many grey levelsSettles in tens of milliseconds (“tens of Hz” in Miscuglio et al.'s comparison)High pixel counts; the usual choice for phase masks
Digital micromirror device (DMD)Amplitude (on/off mirrors; grey levels by time-multiplexing)2 million mirrors at 1,031 Hz with 8-bit depth, about 20 kHz binary, in Miscuglio et al.'s processorAt least two orders of magnitude faster than liquid crystal at the same resolution, per the same paper
Integrated modulators (on chip)Amplitude or phase per waveguideGHz: Feldmann et al. report operation above 14 GHz, limited by modulators and detectorsDemonstrations have few channels, not millions of pixels
  • The trade: free-space SLMs have millions of pixels but kHz-or-slower updates; integrated modulators run at GHz but have few channels. Throughput is pixels × rate either way, and the converters behind every pixel set the energy.
  • The mask is a weight store. A filter that changes every pass is limited by the mask's update rate; a filter held for many passes (a weight, reused across a batch) is nearly free. Deck 02 turns this into a count of mask rewrites per forward pass. A passive on-chip transform has no mask at all: its transform is fixed when it is made, and any filter values must go on modulators at the electrical clock.
  • Rates and pixel counts move quickly with products; treat these as orders of magnitude and check the device datasheet. Sources: Miscuglio et al. (arXiv:2008.05853), Feldmann et al. (arXiv:2002.00281; Nature 589, 2021).

Detectors and the Phase Problem

Photodetectors are square-law: they measure intensity |E|2, not the field E. A coherent 4f system, or an on-chip transform, computes a signed or complex field, and the detector throws away its sign and phase. Four remedies:

Top: a signed field E across the detector (solid) and a different field, −E (dashed). Bottom: what square-law pixels read, |E|²: identical for both. Sign and phase are gone before the ADC sees anything.

RemedyHowCost
Coherent (homodyne) detectionInterfere the output with a reference beam; the cross term is linear in EA phase-stable reference, extra optics or balanced detectors
BiasAdd a constant so the field stays positive, take √intensity, subtractDynamic range: the bias uses ADC codes
Split passesRun positive and negative parts separately and subtract digitallyTwice the passes and conversions
Phase retrievalRecover phase from intensity-only measurements iterativelyMany iterations; not exact in general
  • Direct phase determination: Macfaden, Gordon and Wilkinson decompose the input and use symmetries of the Fourier transform to determine phase directly from intensity measurements, giving an optical complex-to-complex DFT (Sci. Rep. 7, 2017).
  • Homodyne detection in optical neural networks: Hamerly et al. encode both inputs and weights optically and multiply them by interfering the two at the detectors (photoelectric multiplication, arXiv:1812.07614; Phys. Rev. X 9, 2019).
  • Phase retrieval algorithms are compared in Fienup's classic paper (Appl. Opt. 21, 1982).
  • The deck 03 design charges intensity detection as twice the passes, and coherent detection as one ADC sample per real output.

Pixels, the DFT and Coherent Detection

A pixelated input (an SLM, or one modulator per waveguide) and a pixelated detector turn the lens's continuous transform into, approximately, a discrete Fourier transform: N input pixels, N output samples, each sample the DFT bin times a gentle sinc envelope from the pixel shape.

Top: 16 input pixel values. Bottom: |F(u)|² of the pixel row as a continuous curve (pixelFT), and the 16 detector pixels sampling it at u = k/16 (dots): sinc(k/16) × DFT bin k, checked against the DFT in the tests.

  • The DC term dominates a non-negative input: bin 0 is the sum of every pixel, so it sets the ADC's full scale and squeezes every other bin into the bottom codes. That is the crest-factor penalty of slide 17, and a reason to centre the data (signed input) or block DC with a stop in the Fourier plane.

Coherent (balanced) detection, bin by bin, of a signed 8-pixel input (balanced). A 50:50 coupler mixes the signal E with a local oscillator (LO) of the same laser; the two photodiodes read |E + LO|²/2 and |E − LO|²/2; their difference is 2·Re(E·LO*), linear in E. LO at 0° reads the real part, at 90° the imaginary part. A camera reads only |E|² (grey bars).

Right: a butterfly of couplers with delay lines between stages.   • An on-chip FFT has the converter bill of a free-space one without its millions of parallel pixels; its advantage is speed of reconfiguration and integration.

On a Chip: Slab Lens or Butterfly Mesh

Two families compute a Fourier transform on a chip. Both are 1-D, both take their data from a modulator per waveguide (not an illuminated picture), and both are read by a detector per output.

Both panels transform the same 8 modulated inputs. (a) Slab: the field on the output arc is the continuous transform (slabField, curve); output waveguides sample it (dots), giving the DFT. (b) Mesh: bit-reversed wiring, then log28 = 3 layers of N/2 = 4 butterflies (fftStages); bar height = |value| on each waveguide. Both final outputs equal the DFT (checked).

(a) Diffractive or slab “on-chip lens”(b) Interferometer mesh as FFT butterflies
PhysicsFree diffraction across a planar waveguide region2×2 couplers and fixed phase shifters wired as radix-2 (or radix-4) butterflies
ComputesA continuous transform, sampled by the output waveguides: approximately a DFTAn exact N-point DFT, by construction
ErrorsAberration, sampling, crosstalk between outputsCoupler ratio and phase errors that compound over log2N layers

Common ground

  • Input: one modulator per waveguide, at the electrical clock.
  • 2-D = a pass over rows, a transpose, a pass over columns: separable, like a 2-D FFT.

“Instantaneous”, with a clock

  • Light crosses the chip in picoseconds, but a new vector enters only once per modulator symbol, so throughput is the modulator and detector clock (GHz), times the channels.
  • WDM (one transform per wavelength through the same structure) can in principle multiply it.

Convolution on a Chip

The 4f convolver works on a chip too, by the same theorem: transform, multiply, transform back. The difference is the mask. In free space it is a transparency in the Fourier plane, free once made. On a chip it becomes a row of elements on the waveguides between two transforms.

Eight inputs, a smoothing filter h = (¼, ½, ¼), two slabs (chipConv, tested against a direct cyclic convolution). As in 4f, the second transform is a forward one, so the output is mirrored; on a chip the fix is just wiring the output ports in reverse order.

What sits between the transformsWhat it givesCost
Fixed attenuators and phase shifters, set onceA fixed filter: the chip version of a 4f maskPassive once set; thermal phase shifters draw holding power and need calibration
Modulators, driven per valueA filter that changes with the data or holds learned weightsA conversion per value, as on the input side (slide 22)
Nothing: detect, multiply in electronics, send backAny filterTwo passes and a conversion each way between them

Limits

  • 1-D, and only as long as the port count. Longer convolutions are cut into blocks and stitched together digitally (overlap-add).
  • Circular, since it is a DFT: zero-pad to twice the length to make it linear and causal (slide 06).
  • The link module inference: the paper reports no element between transforms, and does not say where its multiplies run. A convolution there would most likely be the third row: transform, multiply digitally, transform again.

Convolution without a Fourier transform

  • In time: delay lines and couplers (FIR) or ring resonators (IIR) filter a serial optical signal directly, the long-standing tools of photonic signal processing.
  • Wavelengths and dispersion: Xu et al.'s 11 TOPS convolutional accelerator weights copies on different wavelengths and delays them by dispersion (Nature 589, 2021).
  • As a matrix product: Feldmann et al.'s photonic tensor core runs convolutions as matrix-vector products (Nature 589, 2021).

The Link Module Uses a Slab Lens

Of the three on-chip routes, the photonic link module (arXiv:2608.21536, slide 15) uses the diffractive slab. Not a learned, patterned slab like Fu et al.'s, but its plainest form: an open slab whose two curved edges make it a lens, the 2f system of slide 04 drawn on a chip.

What it saysSource
The chip holds “a proprietary passive diffractive device that computes Optical Fourier Transform (OFT)”, cited to a patent among others.paper
That patent, EP4677399A1 “Optical Fourier transform device” (Google Patents), describes “a Fourier Transform slab waveguide where the light is constrained in one (cartesian) dimension (z) and diffracts freely in the other two dimensions (x and y)”.patent
Geometry: “the input and output arrays arranged on the arc of a different circle, on which lies the centre of the other circle”; the port spacing is chosen “so that the output ports sample a single order of the Fourier transform”; for an FFT, “the number of output ports used to extract data must be the same as the number of input ports”.patent
The patent's own contribution: a “stray light region” round the slab, which redirects or absorbs (“light sink”) light that misses the output ports, so it cannot scatter back into them.patent

Four modulated inputs, four outputs, as on the module (geometry schematic, not to scale). Each input's wave crosses the slab and reaches the output arc tilted by an amount set by where the input sits, so it adds a phase that steps by a fixed amount from output to output (dials). Each output adds up the dials it receives (slabPorts, tested against the DFT). With four ports the steps are quarter turns, so the only weights are 1, −j, −1 and j.

paper The light routing round the slab, as the paper describes it. A laser feeds seven channels. Four go through the Mach–Zehnder modulators (MZM) and thermo-optic phase shifters (PS) into the slab. One becomes the reference (local oscillator, LO): a slow MZI sets its phase and level, it splits four ways, and each copy beats against one slab output on a balanced photodiode pair. Two feed monitors: mixed with taps from the modulators, read slowly by a microcontroller that sets the phase shifters (dashed loop). The paper's word for the whole is “phase balanced”.

Why a slab, not the other two inference

  • Nothing to tune in the transform. An MZI mesh needs N(N−1)/2 phase shifters held in calibration; the slab's transform is fixed by its shape. The tuned parts (the PS above) only trim the phases going in and the reference. The paper: “Since the core compute technology is passive, the architecture also scales with the Silicon Photonics roadmap for higher data rates per lane.” paper
  • Space, not time. A coupler-and-delay FFT transforms one serial stream over successive symbols (optical OFDM). The module transforms four parallel lanes in one symbol, across space.

Four in, four out: what it implies inference

  • Four modulated inputs and four outputs read against the reference is a 4-point DFT per symbol: one radix-4 butterfly. That matches the paper's “Radix-4 decimation” and “four optical channels”.
  • Its weights are only 1, −j, −1 and j, so binary inputs give whole-number outputs (re and im each between −4 and 4). Those can be read and rounded exactly. For example, X1 = (x0−x2) + j(x3−x1): the sums and differences a radix-4 NTT butterfly needs. The FPGA's modular arithmetic does the rest. That is one plausible route to the paper's exact NTT.
  • If so, a 256-point transform is 4 radix-4 passes (256 = 44), with the twiddle multiplies between passes done in the FPGA. The light does the butterflies, not the whole transform.

Precision: ENOB, Noise and Crosstalk

The effective number of bits (ENOB) of an analogue chain is defined from its signal-to-noise-and-distortion ratio, SINAD = 6.02·ENOB + 1.76 dB for a full-scale sine (Walden's ADC survey, IEEE JSAC 1999). It lumps every error source into one number: shot and thermal noise, laser intensity noise, crosstalk between pixels, aberrations, modulator non-linearity, and the converters' own quantisation.

For floating-point workloads the requirement is a noise level, not exactness. With the ADC's full scale set to the output's peak, the relative RMS error is crest × 2−ENOB × 2/√12, where the crest factor is the output's peak-to-RMS ratio. Measured on a causal FFT convolution:

Binary Input and Bit Planes

A link module that accepts only binary (NRZ) input (slide 15) must feed a multi-bit value as bit planes: plane b holds bit b of every value, each plane goes through the same transform, and the outputs are recombined with weights 2b. Linearity makes that exact: T(x) = Σb 2b T(xb).

0.15

Eight 8-bit inputs, a known integer transform (cyclic convolution with kernel h = [3, 1, 4, 1, 5, 9, 2, 6], which a 4f system, a pair of on-chip transforms with modulators between them, or an FFT computes), and Gaussian readout noise σ per plane (bitPlaneRun). Each plane's outputs are integers from 0 to Σh = 31, so rounding them needs only about 5 bits of clean readout, while the full result spans 13 bits. As σ grows towards a quarter of an output unit, rounding starts to fail and the recombined result is wrong; the panel counts the wrong outputs.

inference  not stated in the paper

With binary input and a known transform, each plane's output only needs reading accurately enough to round. Add error correction and modular arithmetic in the FPGA, and that is a plausible way an exact NTT can run on analogue optics. This is the deck's reading of the published parts list, not a description from the paper. It also shows why the same trick does not help floats (slide 18): there the result is not an integer to round to.

Static power never sleeps

  • Lasers burn power whether or not work arrives; wall-plug efficiency sets the electrical cost.
  • Thermal tuning holds resonant and interferometric devices on wavelength.
  • SLM drivers and their frame buffers in free space; modulator drivers and TIAs on a chip.
  • Static power must be amortised over work: an idle optical engine is pure cost. This is energy proportionality, from the sister series (LLM Inference Simulators 07, “Energy Proportionality: Static Power and Load”).
  • The break-even question is how many digital operations each conversion replaces. A length-L FFT convolution does about 10 log2(2L) + 6 FLOPs per output sample digitally (a forward and an inverse real FFT of length 2L, 2.5·2L·log2(2L) each, and the pointwise product, all divided over L outputs; the filter's spectrum is precomputed), against one DAC and one ADC sample optically. Deck 02 works it through per token: the break-even is near ENOB 12 for the illustrative coefficients.
  • Optical energy per MAC can scale down with problem size, because one optical pass reuses each converted value many times: Anderson et al. find optical energy per MAC scaling as 1/d with Transformer width d in their simulations (arXiv:2302.10360). Large, reused operations are where optics competes.

Transformers' Linear Algebra and Optics

Fourier optics computes one fixed linear map, the Fourier transform, whether it is built as a lens in free space or as a passive transform device on a chip (the link module's, 15). A free-space 4f system adds one pointwise multiply, a mask in the Fourier plane; on a chip that multiply needs a row of modulators between a transform and an inverse transform (slide 14), or else it happens after detection, in electronics. A transformer layer needs something else: learned weight matrices, products of two data streams, and nonlinear steps between them. Follow one layer through, step by step, and see which steps light could take.

Each step is tagged by what optics could do with it: FT a transform or convolution (Fourier optics: a lens or an on-chip transform; for a convolution, 4f + mask, or transform, modulators, inverse transform on a chip), MM a matrix multiply by learned weights or by other data (needs a programmable matmul, which is not Fourier optics), NL nonlinear or data × data (must leave the optics). Every crossing between light and electronics is a DAC or ADC per value. Consecutive optical steps are assumed to stay in light, which is optimistic. The sequence is computed by opticalPlan and checked by a node test. Untick the box: a standard decoder then has nothing for optics to do at all.

Left: a circulant matrix (every row a shift of one vector) is exactly what a transform, a pointwise multiply and an inverse transform apply: a 4f system with a mask, or on a chip two transform devices with a row of modulators between them; the check computes Wx both ways (circulant, circApplyFFT). Right: a general learned matrix needs a programmable mesh: two unitary meshes of N(N−1)/2 Mach–Zehnder interferometers and N attenuators, N² settings in all (Reck/Clements; mziCount).

1 · The convolution route

Transform, pointwise multiply, inverse transform (4f + mask in free space; two transforms with modulators between them on a chip) multiplies by a circulant matrix, so it helps only if the weights are circulant. With block-circulant weights the transform work is 13.99% of prefill FLOPs at 2,048 tokens, or 84.54% (Amdahl bound 6.469x) with the LM head on the last token only. That is speculative: no published LLM uses such weights (deck 02, “Structured Weights: Circulant and Monarch”).

The mask is the weights: 269 mask rewrites per Hyena-2 forward pass at 2,048 tokens take 261 ms on an 8-bit micromirror device (1.03 kHz), against 59.033 ms for a GPU's whole prefill (deck 02, “Holding the Weights: Fourier-Plane Mask Capacity”). On a chip the frame-rate limit goes: the filter values are driven onto modulators at the electrical clock. But each one is another DAC sample per value per pass, and a chip offers tens to hundreds of channels, not millions of pixels. A passive transform device, like the link module's, has no place for the multiply in the light at all; the paper does not say how its multiply and MAC operations map onto the optics.

2 · Models that mix by Fourier transform

FNet and Hyena put a real transform in the layer, but it is what makes them cheap, so it is a small share: 0.09% of prefill FLOPs for FNet-shaped mixing and 0.20% for Hyena-2 (Amdahl bound 1.002x) at 2,048 tokens. The projections and the MLP are the rest (deck 02, “The Amdahl Analysis: Prefill FLOP Shares”).

3 · General optical matmul exists, but it is not Fourier optics

Programmable MZI meshes apply a weight matrix through its SVD (Shen et al., arXiv:1610.02365); wavelength-multiplexed crossbars hold the weights in phase-change cells (Feldmann et al., arXiv:2002.00281). They pay the same bills: a conversion per value, analogue precision, and weight reloads. With this series' illustrative converter figures, conversions break even with digital FFT work at about ENOB 12.0 (deck 02, “Precision and Energy per Token”).

4 · Nonlinear steps leave the optics

Softmax, LayerNorm or RMSNorm, the activations and the data × data gates cannot be done by a fixed linear optical system. Each one forces a trip out of the light and back, so a layer pays conversions both ways several times (the animation counts them), and every conversion is a DAC or ADC sample the optics must win back (slide 20).

paper  5 · the link module's own operation set

The photonic link module of slide 15 lists MAC and floating-point MAC among its operations, alongside the FFT and NTT. Its future-work section projects “45 GFLOPS of FP4 (E2M1) MAC … on a 112 GBaud/s photonic transceiver link for an additional 1 pJ/bit” (arXiv:2608.21536). That is a projection for a future chip, not a measurement, and it is MAC only.

A Fixed Band Is a Mask; Top-k Is a Decision

Why this slide is here

It joins this deck's optics to the question the Compute in Transit series asks: could a link module like 15's compress data while the data crosses it, for free?

  • The traffic. In distributed training, workers swap gradient (or momentum) updates every step, and the wire is often the bottleneck. Compressors such as DeMo take a frequency transform (a DCT) of each chunk and send only its top-k coefficients: the k largest, wherever they fall.
  • The snag for optics. The transform is the part optics does well. Choosing the k largest is not: it needs every coefficient detected, converted and compared, and the module's operation set has no comparisons. Top-k also has to send each value's position (its index) next to it, since the positions change every step.
  • The optical alternative. Keep a fixed band instead: the same bins every step. In a Fourier plane that is just an aperture (the mask of slide 05), so the optics does it at no cost. Both ends know the bins in advance, so no indices are sent. That is the series' goal: compression on the wire without the indices, done for free in the link.
  • The price. A fixed band can miss most of the energy when the energy is not where the band is. Whether training still works is an experimental question, and the last bullet below gives the answer: it does not, by a wide margin.
  • A caveat about this module inference. “An aperture” assumes one transform as wide as the chunk. If the module's slab computes 4-point butterflies (slide 16), then a band of a 256-point transform needs four passes, with digital twiddle multiplies between them. Pruned to the band's outputs, that is still far cheaper than top-k: no comparisons and no indices. But it is not simply free.

Successive 64-sample chunks of a smooth random signal (chunk), their bin energies, and two ways to keep k = 4 bins: the fixed low band (shaded: the same aperture every time) and top-k (orange: wherever the energy is). Top-k captures at least as much energy on every chunk (checked), but finding it needs every bin detected and compared.

  • A fixed band is a fixed aperture, so in optics it is free: no comparisons, no data-dependent control, which matches the module's operation set (slide 15).
  • Top-k depends on the data, so it needs every coefficient detected, converted and compared: digital work after the optics.
  • What it costs in training: in the fixed-band experiment (DeMo on a 15.8M-parameter GPT, 2-D 64×64 chunks), the low band captured 0.013 of the momentum's energy against top-k's 0.643, and final validation loss was 3.158 ± 0.009 against 1.785 ± 0.007 for top-k (results.md at b1f62d3). The Compute in Transit hub has the rest.

A Warning: Two Optical Domains in One Module

The module holds light in two different roles. Inside the chip the transform happens across space: a pattern over waveguides at one instant. On the fibre the data travels in time: one bit after another, NRZ.

A warning: don't confuse the two

“Fourier transform” and “spectrum” mean different things in the two domains. In the chip, the transform is of the data, taken across the waveguides. On the fibre, the spectrum is of the bit stream in time, and says nothing about the data's frequencies. So a band of the data cannot be picked out by filtering the light on the fibre: it has to be chosen in the chip, or at the source, before the bits are serialised.

A 256-bit NRZ stream (16 samples a bit) with a brick-wall mask applied to its spectrum, the cut-off stepped down from 2× to 0.3× the bit rate (lowpass, eye). Left: the first 24 bits. Middle: the eye (every 2-bit window overlaid). Right: the spectrum and the mask. Below the bit rate, inter-symbol interference closes the eye and decisions at the centre start to fail.

The fibre's spectrum is the bits'

Filtering the line signal does not filter the data: it smears neighbouring bits into each other (inter-symbol interference). One flipped exponent bit wrecks a float: 1.0 becomes …

One mode, no picture

A single-mode fibre carries one spatial mode, so a Fourier-plane pattern cannot be sent down it. The transform's output must be detected and re-serialised first.

Saving link time

Filtering in transit does not free link time: the bits still cross the wire. The saving comes from sending fewer samples at the source (Nyquist): transform, keep the band, then transmit.

What to Take Away

  • A lens is a Fourier transformer; two lenses and a mask are a convolver. The transform is free; the mask holds the weights. The spectrum sits one focal length behind the lens, at x = λfu, so the light must be one wavelength and collimated (04, 07). That is the free-space picture; the chip version follows.
  • FFT convolution is circular unless zero-padded to twice the length, which is what makes it causal (the interactive, slide 06).
  • Detectors see intensity: sign and phase need coherent detection, a bias, split passes or retrieval. Balanced photodiodes against a local oscillator read the real and imaginary parts directly; a pixelated detector reads a DFT whose DC term eats the dynamic range (11).
  • On a chip the transform is 1-D: a slab lens samples a continuous transform, a butterfly mesh computes an exact DFT; throughput is the modulator clock times the channels (13). Convolution works too: a row of attenuators, phase shifters or modulators between two transforms is the chip's mask (14).
  • A real link module does it with a passive slab lens, four binary-input channels and coherent detection, and reports no precision, latency or energy per operation (15, 16). Its transform is fixed: a fixed band is chosen by which outputs are read, and a multiply needs modulators between two transforms, or the digital side.
  • Float workloads need a noise level, not exactness: about 8.0 ENOB for INT8-like error and 10.3 for BF16-like; averaging buys bits at 4× passes per bit, and digit planes do not help. Exact integer results are a different game: binary planes need only be read well enough to round (19, an inference).
  • Conversions and static power are the bill. Optics pays only when each converted value is reused by many operations and the engine is kept busy. Free space is wide but slow to change; a chip is fast but narrow and fixed (20, 21).
  • A transformer layer is mostly learned matmuls and nonlinear steps, not transforms. Fourier optics, by lens or on a chip, is one fixed linear map: it helps only circulant weights (speculative) or Fourier-mixing layers (a small share); general matmul needs a programmable mesh, which is not Fourier optics; and every nonlinear step forces a conversion out and back (22).
  • A fixed band is a free mask; top-k is a digital decision. That is why the Compute in Transit series tests a fixed band: no comparisons and no indices, at the price of the energy it misses (23).
  • A warning: the fibre is a different domain. Its spectrum is the bits', not the data's, so filtering it causes inter-symbol interference; to save link time, transform and keep the band before the wire (24).
  • Next: deck 02 asks where LLM inference has transforms to offer, and how much of the work they are.
A caution worth keeping

McMahon's review of optical computing argues that the speed of light is not what gives optics an advantage; any advantage comes from combining several of optics' features (parallelism, passive linear operations, low-loss data movement) while avoiding the pitfalls of conversion and precision (arXiv:2308.00088). The sister series makes the same point about power (LLM Inference Simulators 07, “Power in Photonic and Novel Compute”).
