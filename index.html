Fourier Optics for Inference — Presentation 05

AI Workloads on a Stacked Photonic Chip

Which AI workloads could a future dense, 3D-stacked photonic chip run well, and under what conditions? The future system its developer has described in public stacks a photonic layer on its logic. A simulator now models it: throughput against an all-digital ASIC, energy per useful operation, laser and heat limits, calibration, and an approximate mode that chains optical stages for FFT- and AI-style work. This deck asks what precision AI formats need, what the chip's approximate mode delivers, what each costs in energy, and runs the candidate workloads through one screen. Every number comes from the simulator's data, from tested code in this repository, or from a cited source; every mechanism has a figure driven by a tested model.

The Question, and What Changed

Decks 01–03 asked what a Fourier-optical engine costs in general; deck 04 and the simulator after it asked what today's pluggable photonic link module (arXiv:2608.21536) does. What changed: the simulator (plms 0.3.0) now models the future system its developer has described in public, a photonic layer stacked on its logic, and runs it against an all-digital ASIC of the same logic and of the same silicon. This deck asks which AI workloads that chip could run well, and under what conditions.

Today: a pluggable module

Four optical channels on a QSFP56 module beside a host FPGA; the devices “can operate at 14 GBaud/s (28 Gbps PAM4)” (the paper, §II); the series' simulator found it slower and far costlier in energy than the FPGA alone: 35779 pJ against 56 pJ per full-width transform. modelled

The future chip: stacked

U optical transform units bonded onto the logic that drives them, with W wavelengths, so there is no package port between optics and logic: the 3D stacking is the developer's public statement. The paper's §V describes a monolithic chip, “a 112 GBaud/s Photonic Integrated circuit (PIC)” with “custom high-speed logic operating at 28 GHz”; the simulator takes the 112 GBd and 28 GHz from there. Two modes: exact (integers, for FHE and PQC) and approximate (a few effective bits, for FFT- and AI-style work). public-statement paper

What the simulator models and where the full method is explained: the explainer site's method and its Part II chapters. This deck uses its results; it does not re-derive them.

The short answer

With the simulator's illustrative parameters, no AI workload screened here pays on this chip. Exact mode is held at the memory wall (1×) and at most near 1.1× when compute binds; approximate mode keeps about 3.2 effective bits and costs 32× a digital butterfly of matched precision. The last slides say what would have to change, by how much.

03

The Memory Wall

The simulator times a CKKS bootstrap kernel by kernel on the chip and on two all-digital ASICs: one with the same logic, one that spends the photonic layer's silicon on more logic. With today's HBM the chip and the ASIC take the same time: 52.97 ms (sparse16) and 70.51 ms (full14), 1×, because HBM binds and the optimal split sends nothing through the optics. When compute binds (unconstrained HBM, 10 mm² of logic), exact arithmetic caps the gain near 1.1×, since the optics remove only the modular adds, and the same silicon spent on logic is faster: the best point reaches 0.9163× of the equal-silicon ASIC.

Exact or Approximate: Where the Conversions Go

Exact mode converts at every stage. Each radix-4 stage is decided, recombined from its bit planes and reduced mod p before its twiddle multiply, so every operand crosses out of the light and back once per stage, as 59 bit planes. Approximate mode carries one operand per symbol and chains S stages optically before the light is read out, so the conversions are shared by S stages. The animation runs a 4,096-point FFT's 6 radix-4 stages; choose the mode and S.

Frames from fftChain(mode, S) (the simulator's approximate-mode chapter has the method); the energy figures in the caption come from the simulator's e16 table (8-bit converters, U = 64, 4 λ, 112 GBd). From S = 1 to S = 6 the energy per butterfly-stage falls from 34.11 to 6.929 pJ (4.923×), and the rate is 59× the exact-mode rate. modelled

05

Precision Available

The simulator runs the chained FFT by Monte Carlo: DAC, S lossy unitary stages with phase error, receiver noise at the design SNR (27.47 dB per level step), ADC. With 8-bit converters and 1 dB per stage the output keeps 2.74 effective bits at S = 1 and 3.198 at S = 6: fewer conversions add less noise. At 3 dB per stage the loss wins instead, 2.445 falling to 1.268. Better converters barely help, because the receiver noise sets the ceiling: 16-bit converters reach 3.22 at S = 6, and only a 40 dB receiver SNR lifts it to 4.691.

Precision Needed

A float workload does not need exact results; the analogue chain need only match the error its number format already makes. The series' rule (deck 01, slide 17) gives the ENOB that matches a format's own rounding error on a long convolution: relative RMS error = crest × 2−ENOB × 2/√12. analysis/precision_needed.py regenerates the series' signal and reproduces its three values exactly (BF16 10.3, INT8 8.0, FP8 E4M3 6.3) before computing the new ones.

Reading it

FP4 and INT4 have never been computed in the series: FP4 E2M1 needs 4.20 ENOB and INT4 3.80 by the series' rule. Approximate mode's best, 3.198 effective bits, is on the scale of FP4's own rounding (2.84 to 3.13 in the same measure), and well short of FP8 (4.97) and BF16 (8.96). Matching is not enough on its own: the chain's error adds to the format's, so an FP4 workload on this chip would carry about twice the error variance of FP4 alone. Our numbers, from analysis/out/results.md.

07

Energy at Matched Precision

Exact mode costs 485.9 pJ per 59-bit 4-point transform against 32.35 pJ in the ASIC. The 708 crossings alone cost 354 pJ; the optics replace only the cheapest part of a butterfly, its modular adds. Approximate mode at S = 6 costs 6.929 pJ per butterfly-stage, against 0.7188 pJ for a digital 8-bit complex butterfly (12 multiplies, 22 adds) and 0.2141 pJ for one at the precision the optics actually deliver (digital energies from “Horowitz, ISSCC 2014, 45 nm”, via Han et al. Table I: 32-bit add 0.1 pJ, multiply 3.1 pJ, scaled by 0.25). Its throughput is 59× the exact-mode rate, but energy per useful operation is 32× the digital figure.

The bar stacks the simulator's components (laser at the wall, phase shifters, conversions, recombination or boundary twiddles, logic) one per step; the digital bar is beside it (method: the energy chapter). HBM traffic is the same for both designs and is left out; per bootstrap it adds 1.07 J to each (sparse16). modelled

08

What Breaks Even

Per butterfly-stage, with S stages chained, b converter bits and R = U·W·B·0.95 symbol slots per second, the simulator's energies reduce to one formula (analysis/breakeven.py, which reproduces every row of its table):

E = (8·b·E_conv + twiddles(b)) / S + (n_lasers·P_laser/η_wall + U·(18 + 8W)·P_π) / R

It wins when E falls below a digital butterfly at the bits the optics deliver. The second term is static: laser and heaters, paid whatever S or E_conv. Where it never pays: whenever that static term alone exceeds the digital butterfly. At the defaults the laser alone is 0.3182 pJ and the heaters 1.175 pJ, against 0.2141 pJ: no conversion energy, however small, rescues it.

Physical Limits: Light and Heat

Light. One 20 dBm laser feeds about 20 unit-wavelengths at a 6 dB margin (U × W = 20 at 4 λ, 26 at 1 λ; 8 and 12 at 10 dB), at 112 GBd with a 30 fF receiver. So the default chip needs 13 lasers, and laser power grows with U × W. Heat. Thermo-optic phase shifters at 10 mW per π hold 0.5 W per unit (1.25 W/mm²); they alone reach the 500 W cap at U = 1000.

Physical Limits: Temperature and Calibration

The logic underneath heats the optics. An idle-to-busy step of the logic (0.5 W/mm²) spans 3.192 K across U = 64 and moves heater phases by up to 0.05947 rad; no unit passes the error target until 2 W/mm². Calibration scales worse: with a controller serving 4 units at once, at least 0.9 of units stay in service up to U = 1,024 at 0.03 rad/√s of drift and U = 256 at 0.1 rad/√s; start-up calibration takes 4096 s for U = 16,384.

Fourier Layers and Circulant Weights

Amdahl decides first. In a Llama-3-8B-shaped model at a 2,048-token prompt, Fourier mixing is a sliver of the work: an FNet-shaped mixer (FNet) is 0.09% of prefill FLOPs, Hyena (order 2; arXiv:2302.10866) long convolutions 0.20%. Only when the weights themselves become transforms (block-circulant, as in CirCNN, arXiv:1708.08917) does the share matter: 13.99% with the LM head charged for every token, 84.54% with it on the last token only, as serving engines do.

One variant per step, from the series' FLOP ledger (flop_share.py, prompt 2,048). Bound = 1/(1 − share). Even the circulant case's 6.5× assumes the optics are free and exact enough; at 3.2 effective bits, and 32× the energy of a matched digital butterfly, neither holds. computed

The FP4 MAC: a Projection

The module paper projects an FP4 MAC on a future link: “45 GFLOPS of FP4 (E2M1) MAC is enabled on a 112 GBaud/s photonic transceiver link for an additional 1 pJ/bit” (the paper, §V) paper. That is a projection for a future chip, not a measurement. The paper adds “Since the core compute technology is passive…”, but not whether the MAC itself is analogue and optical or digital in the logic. The two cases lead to different answers. The paper says only FP4 (E2M1); block scaling, below, is this deck's reading.

FP4 E2M1 has eight magnitudes, 0 to 6 (drawn from FP4_GRID, tested in Python). NVFP4 “applies a fine-grained E4M3 scaling factor to each 16-value micro-block” with “a second-level FP32 scalar applied per tensor” (NVIDIA); MXFP4 uses blocks of 32 with an E8M0 power-of-two scale (OCP MX v1.0). Whether the projected MAC is block-scaled is not stated: our reading.

If the MAC is analogue

  • Its error must stay below FP4's own: if it is block-scaled like NVFP4 (our reading), about 3.13 effective bits for NVFP4's rounding of one output, before an accumulation over K products adds its own noise.
  • The approximate mode's 3.198 bits are for an FFT chain, not a MAC; no MAC precision is modelled or published.
  • Energy: the paper projects an additional 1 pJ/bit; the simulator's static laser and heater terms (slide 08) are separate.

If the MAC is digital

  • It is an ordinary FP4 MAC in the stacked logic, and the photonics does not take part in it.
  • Then the chip is a digital FP4 accelerator with an optical side-unit, and the question is the logic's, not the optics'.

Unknown, then, and it is the first question for the developer (slide 18). FP4 is not a precision the series has used before; this deck computed it on slide 06.

What to Take Away

  • The memory wall comes first. With today's HBM the stacked chip and an all-digital ASIC take the same time (1×). Compute-bound, exact arithmetic caps the gain near 1.1×, and the same silicon spent on logic does better (0.9163×).
  • Approximate mode keeps about 3 effective bits: 2.74 at S = 1, 3.198 at S = 6, set by receiver noise, not converters.
  • FP4 needs about that much. By the series' rule FP4 E2M1 needs 4.20 ENOB and INT4 3.80; in the simulator's measure FP4-class formats sit at 2.79 to 3.13 effective bits. FP8 and BF16 are out of reach.
  • Energy fails at matched precision. 6.929 pJ per butterfly-stage against 0.2141 digital; the laser alone (0.3182) exceeds the digital figure.
  • The screen: no candidate passes as modelled. Block-circulant weights are the only large transform share (84.54%); the FP4 MAC is a projection whose analogue-ness is unknown; KV and training traffic belong to the link, not the chip.
  • Exact mode, the developer's first market, shows no modelled gain either: 485.9 against 32.35 pJ per transform.
